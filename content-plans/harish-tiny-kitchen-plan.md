# Long-form script 3: The $25 Tiny-Kitchen Grocery Challenge

**Working title:** I Cooked Dinner for Four With a $25 Tiny-Kitchen Grocery Basket

**Alternate titles:**

- What I Can Actually Cook in a Tiny Kitchen for $25
- Budget Grocery Challenge: Four Full Plates, One Small Counter
- A Realistic Low-Cost Dinner That Does Not Feel Low-Cost

**Target runtime:** 12–15 minutes

**Thumbnail:** Receipt, full skillet, and four bowls; text: “$25 / 4 PLATES”

**Important framing:** Prices vary by city and season. The episode shows the local receipt, labels the date, and focuses on the decision-making framework rather than promising a fixed universal price.

### Script

**[0:00–0:25 | Cold open | Grocery receipt and finished food]**

**VISUAL:** Receipt close-up with the total covered by a hand. Cut to four bowls of tomato chickpea curry, rice, and cucumber yogurt. Cut to the tiny counter with every item visible.

**HARISH:**

“Can a tiny kitchen turn a small grocery basket into a filling dinner for four without relying on a secret pantry full of ingredients? Today I’m working with a target of twenty-five dollars. The local price may be different where you live, so I’ll show the receipt and explain every choice. We are making tomato chickpea curry, rice, a quick cucumber yogurt, and a crunchy topping. Four plates, one burner, one main pan, and no expensive shortcut.”

**[0:25–1:15 | What the budget must accomplish]**

**VISUAL:** Ingredient cards: rice, chickpeas, onions, canned tomatoes, carrots, cucumber, yogurt, eggs or optional tofu, greens, lemon, and herbs. Price cards appear as actual local prices.

**HARISH:**

“A budget meal still needs to do four jobs. It needs enough protein to feel satisfying, enough volume to feed everyone, enough contrast that it does not taste flat, and enough leftovers or flexibility that the purchase is worth making. Our protein today is chickpeas and yogurt, with eggs as an optional add-on if the basket allows. Rice brings volume. Tomatoes and onions build the sauce. Cucumber, lemon, and herbs bring freshness.”

“I am choosing ingredients that work across more than one meal. A bag of rice is not only for this dinner. Cucumber can go in the yogurt or tomorrow’s lunch. Carrots can go in the curry, be sliced raw, or be roasted if you have an oven. A good budget basket is a collection of repeatable ingredients, not a single-use recipe.”

**[1:15–2:25 | Grocery haul and substitutions]**

**VISUAL:** Harish unloads the basket, puts the receipt beside it, and sorts items into “base,” “protein,” “fresh,” and “flavor.”

**HARISH:**

“Here is the actual basket. I’m using a store-brand can of chickpeas, store-brand crushed tomatoes, rice, two onions, carrots, a cucumber, plain yogurt, a lemon, and a small bunch of herbs. If you do not have yogurt, use a squeeze of lemon and a little oil over the bowl. If chickpeas are expensive in your area, lentils or beans can replace them. If fresh herbs are not in the budget, use dried herbs or skip them.”

“The most important substitution is not a specific ingredient. It is buying what is affordable today. Compare unit prices when you can, check the frozen vegetable section, and use dry beans if you already have them. This episode is a framework for making a decision under a number, not a challenge to copy my receipt exactly.”

**[2:25–3:35 | Tiny-kitchen mise en place]**

**VISUAL:** Use one bowl for scraps. Rinse rice. Chop onion and carrot. Grate cucumber into a towel or strainer. Measure spices into one small cup.

**HARISH:**

“In a small kitchen, setup prevents waste. I’m putting a bowl beside the cutting board for scraps, rinsing the rice before the cutting board gets crowded, and mixing the spice blend in one cup: cumin, paprika, black pepper, salt, and a little chili if you like heat.”

“I’m grating half the cucumber for yogurt and slicing the other half into thin pieces for the topping. Press out excess water with a clean towel. This makes the yogurt sauce thicker and prevents the bowl from becoming watery.”

“The rice goes into the pot first. Add water and salt, bring it to a boil, then cover and lower the heat. It can cook while the curry starts. Since the counter is narrow, I’m keeping the pot on the burner and doing all the chopping in one short window.”

**[3:35–5:25 | Build the base]**

**VISUAL:** Skillet with oil, onions, carrots, spices, garlic if available, tomatoes. Show the color changes at each stage.

**HARISH:**

“The skillet gets oil and onions. I’m not rushing this part. Even a budget meal can taste deep if the onions have time to soften and take on color. Add the carrots once the onion is translucent. They bring sweetness and make the sauce feel generous.”

“Now the spices go in. Watch how the oil changes color. This is a small step, but it is where the aroma wakes up. If your spices smell bitter or smoky, the heat is too high; add the tomatoes immediately.”

“Add the tomatoes, rinse the can with a little water, and pour that in too. The water brings the last tomato flavor into the pan and gives the chickpeas enough liquid to simmer. Scrape the bottom and bring the mixture to a steady bubble.”

**HARISH, on camera:**

“Taste the sauce before the chickpeas go in. At this point it may taste sharp or unfinished. That is okay. Tomatoes become rounder as they cook, and the chickpeas will add starch and body.”

**[5:25–7:15 | Add chickpeas and explain budget volume]**

**VISUAL:** Chickpeas added. Harish mashes a quarter of them against the pan. Carrots and greens added. Lid on.

**HARISH:**

“Add the drained chickpeas. I’m going to mash about a quarter of them against the side of the skillet. Whole chickpeas give us bite; mashed chickpeas thicken the sauce. This is how we make a modest amount of protein feel substantial without adding cream or another expensive ingredient.”

“Add the remaining carrots and any greens now. Cover for five minutes so the vegetables soften and the chickpeas absorb the sauce. The rice should be nearly finished, so turn off the burner and leave the pot covered.”

**VISUAL:** Lift lid, stir, show sauce coating chickpeas. Add lemon juice and taste.

**HARISH:**

“Now taste again. It needs enough salt to make the tomatoes taste like tomatoes, and lemon to lift the whole pan. Add acid in small amounts. If it tastes flat, lemon is often more useful than more spice.”

**[7:15–8:35 | Cucumber yogurt and crunchy topping]**

**VISUAL:** Mix yogurt, cucumber, lemon zest or juice, salt, and herbs. Slice onion or carrot very thin. Toast optional seeds or spices in the warm pan after curry is transferred.

**HARISH:**

“While the curry rests, make the cooling side. Yogurt, squeezed cucumber, lemon, salt, and herbs. This takes less than a minute and gives the meal contrast. We have a warm, saucy base, so the side should be cold and fresh.”

“For crunch, I’m slicing a few onion and carrot pieces very thin. If you have seeds or nuts already in the pantry, toast a spoonful in the warm skillet after the curry is transferred. If not, the raw vegetables are enough. Budget cooking is not about adding a substitute for every expensive ingredient; it is about noticing where texture is missing and solving it with what you have.”

**[8:35–9:45 | Plate four servings]**

**VISUAL:** Use a measuring cup or spoon to portion rice evenly. Add curry, yogurt, vegetables, and herbs to four bowls. Show the servings side by side.

**HARISH:**

“Let’s plate four servings evenly. Rice first, then chickpea curry, then a spoon of cucumber yogurt, then the crunchy vegetables. Portioning before everyone starts eating keeps one bowl from accidentally becoming enormous while another gets mostly sauce.”

“Look at the plate: grain, legumes, vegetables, cooling yogurt, and acid. It is colorful because we planned for contrast, not because we bought a dozen ingredients.”

**[9:45–10:55 | Taste test and cost breakdown]**

**VISUAL:** Harish tastes. Overlay a simple table with local receipt amounts and portions. Show cost per serving, clearly labeled as approximate.

**HARISH:**

“The curry is rich enough to feel like a full dinner, but the lemon keeps it from feeling heavy. The chickpeas have texture because we only mashed some of them. The cucumber yogurt is doing more than cooling the spice; it makes the rice and sauce feel like a complete plate.”

“On this receipt, the basket came to [INSERT LOCAL TOTAL]. The main dinner ingredients used today came to approximately [INSERT USED TOTAL], or about [INSERT PER-SERVING TOTAL] per portion. Those numbers change by location, season, and what you already have at home. The important calculation is not whether your receipt matches mine. It is whether each purchase can work in more than one meal.”

**[10:55–12:15 | Leftovers and second meal]**

**VISUAL:** Put leftover curry in a container. Next day, spoon it into a wrap or over toast/rice. Add water to transform into soup.

**HARISH:**

“The leftovers can become tomorrow’s lunch. Warm the curry with a splash of water and serve it over rice again, or fold it into a wrap with the cucumber yogurt. You can also loosen it into a soup and add the last carrot pieces. Store cooked food promptly in shallow containers and reheat until steaming.”

“If you are cooking for one, make the same recipe but store the rice and curry separately. Freeze one portion if you know you will not eat it within the next few days. A budget meal becomes more valuable when it prevents an expensive emergency takeaway later.”

**[12:15–13:05 | Close]**

**HARISH, on camera:**

“The challenge worked because the basket had a base, a protein, a fresh element, and multiple jobs for each ingredient. The tiny kitchen made us sequence carefully, but it did not make the dinner feel small.”

“If you have a grocery target you want me to test, leave the number and your city or country. I can build the method around the constraint. Subscribe for more realistic small-space cooking, and I’ll see you in the next one.”

**END SCREEN:** “What should the next budget be? Comment your number.”

### Description draft

“This is a realistic tiny-kitchen grocery challenge using a local receipt and a target of $25. I make tomato chickpea curry, rice, cucumber yogurt, and a crunchy vegetable topping for four servings. Prices vary, so use the substitutions and budgeting framework rather than treating the total as universal.”

### Pinned comment

“Prices differ everywhere. Tell me your grocery target and the ingredient you already have in your pantry; I’ll use the replies to design the next challenge.”

# Long-form script 4: Cooking for Guests in a Tiny Kitchen

**Working title:** I Cooked a Three-Course Dinner for Guests in My Tiny Kitchen

**Alternate titles:**

- How to Host in a Tiny Kitchen Without Panicking
- Small Kitchen Dinner Party: Three Courses, One Burner
- Guests Are Coming: A Calm Tiny-Kitchen Hosting Plan

**Target runtime:** 13–16 minutes

**Thumbnail:** Harish holding a finished tray beside a narrow counter; text: “GUESTS IN 60 MIN”

**Menu:** Lemon-herb yogurt dip with crunchy vegetables; one-pan tomato chickpea stew with rice; skillet fruit with yogurt or ice cream.

**Episode promise:** A complete hosting workflow that makes the meal feel special through timing, contrast, and preparation rather than equipment.

### Script

**[0:00–0:25 | Cold open]**

**VISUAL:** Doorbell sound. Harish glances at the clock. Quick cuts of a narrow counter, a single burner, a steaming pan, four bowls, and a small dessert skillet.

**HARISH:**

“My guests arrive in sixty minutes, the kitchen is tiny, and I have one burner. So today we are not trying to cook four complicated restaurant plates at once. We are building a dinner that can wait, share, and recover. There is a cool first course, a saucy main with rice, and a warm fruit dessert. The goal is to host calmly and still sit down with everyone.”

**[0:25–1:20 | Hosting rules]**

**VISUAL:** Three index cards: “make ahead,” “one pan at a time,” “serve family-style.”

**HARISH:**

“Tiny-kitchen hosting has three rules. First, make at least one course completely ahead. Second, never require two hot pans at the same moment if you only have one burner. Third, serve family-style whenever possible. Family-style food looks generous, and it means you are not trapped behind the counter plating four identical portions.”

“Our first course is yogurt dip with lemon, herbs, cucumber, and raw vegetables. It can sit in the fridge. The main is tomato chickpea stew over rice. It gets better as it rests. Dessert is sliced fruit warmed with cinnamon and lemon, served with yogurt or ice cream. Nothing depends on a perfect last-second flourish.”

**[1:20–2:30 | Reset the kitchen]**

**VISUAL:** Clear counter, empty sink, trash bowl, towel under cutting board, tools arranged vertically. Show the small footprint.

**HARISH:**

“Before cooking, reset the room. Empty the sink, wipe the counter, put a scrap bowl beside the board, and place the serving dishes where they will live. In a tiny kitchen, every dish you leave in the way becomes an obstacle later.”

“I’m using one pot for rice, one wide skillet for the main, one small bowl for the dip, and one knife. The serving plates are already out. I also put a clean towel under the cutting board so it does not slide. These are not glamorous steps, but they protect the calm part of the evening.”

**[2:30–4:10 | First course: lemon-herb yogurt dip]**

**VISUAL:** Grate cucumber, squeeze it dry, mix yogurt, lemon, garlic, herbs, salt, and pepper. Arrange carrots, cucumber, and crackers or flatbread.

**HARISH:**

“Start with the course that can wait. Grate cucumber and squeeze out the water. Stir it into yogurt with lemon, a little garlic, salt, pepper, and herbs. Taste it cold, because the final flavor will be served cold. It should be bright and slightly more seasoned than you think; vegetables and flatbread will soften that intensity.”

“For the platter, use whatever is crisp: carrots, cucumber, radishes, bell pepper, crackers, or flatbread. Cut the vegetables into pieces that can be picked up with one hand. Guests should not need a knife for the first course.”

**VISUAL:** Spoon dip into a shallow bowl, make a swirl with the back of a spoon, add oil, herbs, and pepper. Cover and refrigerate.

**HARISH:**

“The swirl is optional, but a shallow bowl makes a small amount feel abundant. A little oil and herbs on top tell your guests this was intentional. Cover it and put it in the fridge. One course is done before the burner even turns on.”

**[4:10–5:30 | Start rice and prep the main]**

**VISUAL:** Rinse rice, measure water, start pot. Chop onions and carrots while rice begins. Open chickpeas and tomatoes.

**HARISH:**

“Now the rice. Rinse it, add water and salt, bring it to a boil, cover, and lower the heat. Once the lid is on, the pot needs very little attention. That is exactly what we want.”

“While it starts, chop onions and carrots for the main. I’m using chickpeas today because they can simmer without drying out, and because a saucy main is forgiving when guests arrive a few minutes late. If you eat meat, chicken thighs work too, but cook them fully before adding the tomatoes.”

**[5:30–7:45 | Build the main]**

**VISUAL:** Skillet with oil, onions, carrots, garlic, spices, tomatoes, chickpeas. Use a close-up for the sauce consistency.

**HARISH:**

“The skillet gets oil, onions, and a pinch of salt. Once they soften, add carrots and garlic. The spices go in next: cumin, paprika, black pepper, and a small amount of chili. Bloom them in the oil, then add tomatoes and a splash of water.”

“Let the sauce simmer for a few minutes before adding chickpeas. This gives the tomatoes time to lose their raw edge. Add the chickpeas, lower the heat, and let the pan become thick and glossy.”

**HARISH, on camera:**

“This is the host-friendly part of the menu. If it finishes ten minutes early, it is still good. If someone is late, it can stay on low heat with a lid. If the sauce gets too thick, add water. We want a meal that can absorb real life.”

**VISUAL:** Add greens, lemon, and herbs. Taste and adjust.

**HARISH:**

“Add greens near the end so they keep their color. Taste for salt, then add lemon. Acid makes a big pan taste awake. Turn off the heat and keep the lid on. The rice should be resting now too.”

**[7:45–8:55 | Dessert prep]**

**VISUAL:** Slice apples, pears, bananas, or stone fruit. Mix with cinnamon, lemon, and a pinch of sugar or honey. Keep in a bowl.

**HARISH:**

“Dessert is prepared before the guests arrive but cooked after the main, so the kitchen smells warm at the end of the meal. Slice fruit into similar-sized pieces. Toss with cinnamon, lemon, and a small spoon of sugar or honey if needed.”

“If your fruit is already very sweet, skip the sugar. The yogurt or ice cream adds the creamy sweetness later. Put the fruit beside the skillet, not in it yet. We are protecting our one hot surface for the main.”

**[8:55–10:05 | Table and guest arrival transition]**

**VISUAL:** Set water, napkins, serving spoons, dip, and vegetables. Quick time-lapse of the kitchen staying tidy. Doorbell.

**HARISH, voice-over:**

“The table does not need to be elaborate. Put out water, napkins, the dip, vegetables, and one serving spoon. When the first course is ready to eat, guests can start while the main rests. That gives you a natural buffer.”

**DOORBELL.**

**HARISH, on camera at door:**

“Welcome. The first course is ready, and I’m going to finish the main while we talk.”

**[10:05–11:20 | Serve the first course and finish the main]**

**VISUAL:** Guests’ hands take vegetables. Harish fluffs rice and opens the skillet. Show separate serving bowls.

**HARISH, voice-over:**

“This is why the first course was cold. I can bring it to the table immediately and still have a few minutes to check the main. Fluff the rice with a fork. If it seems dry, do not add lots of water; add a spoon of the tomato sauce when serving.”

“Open the skillet and check the consistency. The sauce should cling to the chickpeas but still move when the pan tilts. If it is too thick, add a splash of water. If it is loose, cook uncovered for another minute.”

**HARISH, on camera:**

“I’m transferring the stew to a serving bowl instead of leaving it on the burner. That frees the skillet for dessert and makes the table look like dinner has arrived. Add lemon wedges and herbs at the table so the colors stay fresh.”

**[11:20–12:25 | Family-style main]**

**VISUAL:** Rice bowl, chickpea stew, lemon wedges, yogurt, herbs placed on table. Guests serve themselves.

**HARISH:**

“Rice in the middle, stew beside it, lemon wedges, and a little yogurt. Let everyone serve themselves. A generous bowl in the center is less stressful than four plates that all need to look identical.”

“The food is warm, saucy, and forgiving. The rice gives the table a base, the chickpeas bring protein, and the lemon and yogurt let each person adjust the bowl. When you host, flexibility is part of hospitality.”

**[12:25–13:25 | Dessert in the skillet]**

**VISUAL:** After clearing only the essential dishes, warm the skillet. Add fruit, cinnamon, and a small splash of water. Cook until edges soften. Serve with yogurt or ice cream.

**HARISH:**

“After the main, I’m clearing only what we need. The skillet goes back on the burner. Add the fruit, cinnamon, and a small splash of water. Cook until the edges soften and the pan smells like dessert.”

“This takes just a few minutes. Stir gently so the pieces keep their shape. Spoon the warm fruit over yogurt or ice cream. If you have nuts or granola, add them for crunch. If not, the warm-and-cool contrast is already enough.”

**VISUAL:** Spoon cuts through warm fruit and cold yogurt. Guests taste. Harish sits down.

**HARISH:**

“This is the payoff: a warm dessert from the same skillet, without a second appliance or a complicated finish. The kitchen can be small and the meal can still have a beginning, middle, and end.”

**[13:25–14:35 | Hosting debrief and close]**

**VISUAL:** Empty serving bowls, small stack of dishes, clean counter. Show a written timeline.

**HARISH:**

“The hosting timeline was: reset the kitchen, make the dip, start the rice, build the main, prep the dessert, set the table, serve the cold course, finish and serve the main, then warm the fruit. Every stage had a place to wait. That is the secret.”

“When the kitchen is tiny, do not choose dishes that all demand attention at once. Choose one cool course, one forgiving main, and one quick finish. Give yourself a clean counter and a serving bowl. Those details make the evening feel bigger than the room.”

“If you host in a small kitchen, tell me the dish that causes the most stress. Subscribe for more practical cooking without a giant kitchen, and I’ll see you at the next meal.”

**END SCREEN:** “Small kitchen, full table. Comment your hosting challenge.”

### Description draft

“A complete tiny-kitchen hosting plan for a three-course meal: lemon-herb yogurt dip, tomato chickpea stew with rice, and warm skillet fruit. The menu is designed around one burner, family-style serving, make-ahead elements, and food that can wait without falling apart.”

### Pinned comment

“Hosting rule: make one course ahead, make one main forgiving, and make dessert fast. What would you serve in your tiny kitchen?”

## 1. Channel foundation

### 1.1 Positioning

Harish Tiny Kitchen is a practical cooking channel for people who have a small kitchen, limited time, limited counter space, or all three. The channel makes everyday cooking feel possible rather than performative. Each episode begins with a real constraint—one pan, a short grocery list, a narrow counter, leftovers, a single burner, or dinner for unexpected guests—and ends with a satisfying meal and a repeatable method.

The channel is not built around expensive appliances, perfect kitchens, or restaurant-style plating. The visual world should feel warm, clever, and attainable: a clean but genuinely compact kitchen, ingredients that can be found at an ordinary supermarket, and instructions that explain why a step matters.

**One-sentence promise:** Harish turns tiny-kitchen constraints into generous, flavorful meals that ordinary people can actually repeat.

### 1.2 Audience

**Primary viewer:**

- Age 20–44
- Lives in a studio, apartment, dorm, accessory dwelling, or shared home
- Cooks for one to four people
- Wants affordable meals but does not want bland “survival food”
- Searches for practical recipes, meal prep, small-kitchen organization, and low-waste ideas
- Watches on a phone while cooking or during a short break

**Secondary viewers:**

- Students and first-apartment renters
- Newly married couples and young families
- People downsizing or renovating a kitchen
- Home cooks who want fewer dishes
- Viewers who enjoy calm, personality-led food content
- Anyone who likes a clear challenge or experiment

### 1.3 Editorial pillars

1. **Tiny-kitchen transformations:** Make a complete meal with one pan, one burner, one cutting board, or almost no counter space.
2. **Budget and value:** Show the actual basket, portions, leftovers, and substitutions.
3. **Flavor education:** Explain browning, blooming spices, balancing acid, layering texture, and using leftovers intelligently.
4. **Real-life hosting:** Cook for a guest or family without a large kitchen, a long prep list, or panic.
5. **Reset and systems:** Keep ingredients, tools, and cleanup manageable so cooking remains sustainable.

### 1.4 Voice and visual identity

Harish should sound like a capable friend standing beside the viewer: observant, encouraging, lightly funny, and specific. Avoid chef posturing, exaggerated claims, and guilt about convenience foods. Use plain language first, then add the useful technical explanation.

**Voice examples:**

- “The pan is small, so we are going to build flavor in layers instead of trying to crowd everything in at once.”
- “If your onion looks a little darker than mine, that is fine. We are cooking dinner, not taking an exam.”
- “This is the tiny-kitchen rule: every ingredient should earn its space in the fridge.”

**Visual rules:**

- Open with the finished food or the constraint within the first five seconds.
- Keep the camera close enough to see texture: bubbling sauce, a browned edge, a knife passing through a tomato.
- Show the actual size of the work surface at least once in every long-form episode.
- Use on-screen labels for time, cost, pan count, and substitutions.
- Let natural kitchen sounds remain under some transitions: chopping, sizzling, a lid closing.
- Use captions on every vertical video and all key spoken points in long-form videos.

### 1.5 Repeatable episode structure

1. **Cold open (0:00–0:20):** Finished dish, constraint, and a reason to stay.
2. **Promise (0:20–0:45):** What will be cooked and what will be learned.
3. **Set-up (0:45–2:00):** Tools, ingredients, substitutions, and the tiny-kitchen limitation.
4. **Build (2:00–8:30):** Cook in clear stages, explaining decisions.
5. **Payoff (8:30–10:30):** Plate, taste, texture check, and cost/leftover notes.
6. **Practical close (10:30–11:30+):** Cleanup, storage, one takeaway, and a specific next-video invitation.

### 1.6 Series names and thumbnail language

**Series names:**

- *One Burner, Big Dinner*
- *The Tiny Kitchen Challenge*
- *Small Space, Full Plate*
- *Countertop Cooking School*
- *The Five-Ingredient Reset*

**Thumbnail phrases:**

- “ONE PAN / 4 SERVINGS”
- “$25 TINY KITCHEN”
- “GUESTS IN 60 MIN”
- “7 DINNERS / 1 PREP”
- “NO OVEN NEEDED”

Use one food hero, one clearly visible constraint, and no more than four words of large thumbnail text. A hand, pan, or narrow counter can communicate scale better than a generic kitchen photo.

# Long-form script 1: 7 Dinners From One Tiny-Kitchen Grocery Run

**Working title:** I Made 7 Dinners in My Tiny Kitchen From One Small Grocery Run

**Alternate titles:**

- One Grocery Basket, Seven Tiny-Kitchen Dinners
- The Small-Kitchen Meal Plan That Actually Uses Everything
- I Planned a Week of Dinners Without Filling My Fridge

**Target runtime:** 12–14 minutes

**Thumbnail:** A compact grocery basket beside seven labeled meal containers; text: “7 DINNERS / 1 BASKET”

**Recipe system:** Rice, eggs, chickpeas, chicken thighs or tofu, onions, tomatoes, greens, carrots, yogurt, lemons, and one flexible sauce become seven distinct dinners.

**Production note:** Display local prices on screen rather than promising a universal total. The script uses “about” language so the episode stays useful across regions.

### Script

**[0:00–0:20 | Cold open | Fast montage]**

**VISUAL:** A full plate of lemony chicken and rice. Cut to a small grocery basket. Cut to the narrow counter with all ingredients arranged in a single row. Cut to seven sticky notes labeled Monday through Sunday. Cut to Harish lifting a lid from a pan.

**HARISH, on camera:**

“This is my entire grocery run, and it is about to become seven dinners in a kitchen where the cutting board is almost wider than the counter. We are making one smart base, changing the flavor every night, and using every ingredient at least twice. No giant pantry haul, no seven separate recipes, and no mystery container hiding in the back of the fridge. Let’s make a week of food that still feels like dinner.”

**[0:20–0:55 | Promise]**

**VISUAL:** Ingredients appear as labeled overhead cards: rice, eggs, chickpeas, chicken or tofu, onions, garlic, tomatoes, carrots, greens, yogurt, lemons, herbs, and a small spice group.

**HARISH, voice-over:**

“The trick is not cooking seven completely different meals. That would create seven shopping lists and seven piles of dishes. The trick is choosing ingredients that can change jobs. Rice becomes a bowl, a fried-rice base, and a quick soup. Yogurt becomes a sauce and a marinade. Chickpeas can be crisp, saucy, or mashed. Today I’ll prep the pieces once, then show you the seven dinners and the exact moment each one becomes different.”

**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** “One prep session • 7 dinners • flexible for meat, tofu, or beans”

**HARISH, on camera:**

“Use the ingredient list as a template, not a rulebook. If chicken is expensive, use tofu or extra chickpeas. If you have frozen spinach, use that. The important thing is the method: one grain, one protein, two vegetables, one cooling sauce, and a few flavor switches.”

**[0:55–2:00 | The grocery run and the small-space setup]**

**VISUAL:** Harish places a cloth under the cutting board to stop it sliding. The camera shows the tiny sink, one burner, one skillet, one pot, one knife, and two storage containers.

**HARISH:**

“Here is the equipment: one medium pot, one wide skillet, one knife, one cutting board, a mixing bowl, and storage containers. In a tiny kitchen, the order matters as much as the recipe. I’m going to wash the produce first, clear the sink, and keep a bowl nearby for scraps so the work surface stays usable.”

“The grocery basket has rice, eggs, chickpeas, boneless chicken thighs today, but tofu works exactly the same way, onions, garlic, carrots, tomatoes, a bag of greens, plain yogurt, two lemons, and herbs. The flavor shelf is salt, pepper, paprika, cumin, chili flakes, and a curry-style spice blend. You can substitute the spices you already have. We are building a system, not asking you to buy a new spice rack.”

**[2:00–4:15 | Prep once]**

**VISUAL:** Overhead prep montage, with portions placed in small bowls. Show the small counter filling, then consolidate.

**HARISH, voice-over:**

“First, the prep that will save us later. I’m slicing one onion. Half will be cooked slowly for the base, and half will stay raw for crunch. I’m chopping the carrots into two shapes: small dice for the rice and thin ribbons for a salad. This takes one extra minute now and prevents the ‘I’m too tired to chop’ problem on Wednesday.”

“The tomatoes are getting a rough chop. The greens stay whole until they meet heat so they do not wilt in the fridge. I’m mixing yogurt with lemon zest, lemon juice, salt, and a little grated garlic. Taste it now. It should be bright and a little salty because it will cool down spicy food later.”

**VISUAL:** Harish rinses rice, adds it to the pot with water and salt, and starts it. In the skillet, onion and carrot begin to soften.

**HARISH:**

“I’m cooking a pot of plain rice because a neutral base gives us the most options. While it cooks, the skillet gets onion, carrot, garlic, salt, and a little oil. We want the onion soft and lightly golden, not necessarily dark. Half of this mixture becomes our first dinner; the other half goes into the fridge for tomorrow’s bowl.”

**VISUAL:** Cooked rice spread on a tray or wide plate to cool. Label two containers: “rice” and “onion-carrot base.”

**HARISH:**

“Spread cooked rice out before storing it so steam can escape. Once it is no longer hot, refrigerate it promptly in a shallow container. Keep today’s portions visible. A tiny fridge only works when the food you need is easy to see.”

**[4:15–5:45 | Dinner one: lemon chicken or tofu bowl]**

**VISUAL:** Season chicken or tofu with salt, pepper, paprika, and cumin. Sear in the skillet. Add tomatoes and a splash of water; finish with greens.

**HARISH:**

“Dinner one is the anchor bowl: spiced chicken, or tofu if that is what you eat, over rice with greens and the lemon yogurt. Pat the chicken dry before seasoning. That small step is what lets the surface brown instead of steam.”

“The pan is hot enough when a drop of water skitters and disappears. Add the protein in one layer. In a small skillet, you may need to work in two batches. Crowding is not a personal failure; it is just a capacity issue. Give the first batch space, then move it to a plate.”

“Once the protein is browned, add the chopped tomatoes and a splash of water. Scrape the browned bits. That is free flavor. Add the greens at the very end so they stay green and have some structure.”

**VISUAL:** Plate rice, protein, greens, a spoon of yogurt, raw onion and carrot ribbons. Harish tastes.

**HARISH:**

“The bowl should have four things: something savory, something fresh, something creamy, and something with a little bite. That is why a simple rice bowl feels complete. Save one serving of the protein and two servings of rice for later.”

**[5:45–7:00 | Dinner two: crispy chickpea skillet]**

**VISUAL:** The next day. Dry chickpeas in a towel, skillet heating, chickpeas rolling and browning.

**HARISH, voice-over:**

“Dinner two uses the same rice and yogurt, but it should not taste like yesterday. Drain chickpeas and dry them well. Moisture is the enemy of crisp edges. Add oil to the skillet, then chickpeas, cumin, paprika, salt, and a pinch of chili. Leave them alone for a minute before stirring.”

**HARISH, on camera:**

“Listen for the change. At first they sound wet and soft. As they crisp, the pan gets louder and the chickpeas move more freely. That sound cue is more reliable than a timer because every pan and burner is different.”

“Serve them over warm rice with the raw carrot ribbons, lemon yogurt, and herbs. Add the leftover roasted or sautéed vegetables. Same base, different texture: crunchy chickpeas instead of a seared protein, fresh ribbons instead of cooked greens.”

**[7:00–8:10 | Dinner three: quick tomato egg rice]**

**VISUAL:** Tomato, onion, garlic, and spices simmer in the skillet. Eggs crack into wells. Lid goes on.

**HARISH:**

“Dinner three is for the evening when you have ten minutes of energy left: tomato egg rice. Warm the onion-carrot base with tomatoes, garlic, paprika, and a little water. Make two wells and crack in the eggs. Cover until the whites are set and the yolks are still soft.”

“If you prefer fully cooked eggs, give them another minute. Put the sauce over rice and finish with yogurt or herbs. This is where a cooked base becomes a completely new meal. The difference comes from changing the sauce and the shape of the protein, not buying more ingredients.”

**VISUAL:** Cut yolk, spoon sauce over rice, close-up of steam.

**HARISH:**

“If the sauce tastes flat, add lemon before adding more salt. Acid wakes up a tired sauce. If it is too sharp, add a spoon of yogurt when it is on the plate.”

**[8:10–9:15 | Dinner four: fried rice reset]**

**VISUAL:** Cold rice, egg, remaining vegetables, greens. Show rice grains separating.

**HARISH, voice-over:**

“Dinner four is the fridge reset. Cold rice is ideal because the grains are dry enough to fry instead of becoming porridge. Push the vegetables to one side, scramble an egg in the open space, then fold everything together.”

**HARISH:**

“Use whatever seasoning you have: soy sauce, a little salt and lemon, or a spoon of chili crisp. Add sauce around the hot edge of the pan, not directly onto one pile of rice. It hits the heat, becomes fragrant, and spreads more evenly.”

“This meal is a good place for the last handful of greens and the carrot pieces that are too small for anything else. Tiny-kitchen cooking is partly a flavor skill and partly a visibility skill: if you can see the ingredients, you are more likely to use them.”

**[9:15–10:15 | Dinner five: chickpea and tomato soup]**

**VISUAL:** Pot with onion base, tomato, chickpeas, water or stock, rice stirred in at the end. Steam and spoon shot.

**HARISH:**

“Dinner five is a soup built from the same pantry. Add tomatoes, chickpeas, water or stock, cumin, and a small spoon of the cooked rice. Simmer until it thickens slightly. The rice gives the broth body without needing cream.”

“Finish with lemon and herbs. If you have yogurt left, swirl it in the bowl rather than the hot pot so it stays smooth. This is a useful formula: legume, tomato, grain, acid. You can change the spice profile and make it yours.”

**[10:15–11:10 | Dinner six: warm salad and dinner seven: leftovers remix]**

**VISUAL:** Warm chickpeas and vegetables folded with greens. Next day, leftover rice pressed into a pan and topped with a fried egg or remaining protein.

**HARISH:**

“Dinner six is a warm salad: greens, carrots, chickpeas, the last cooked vegetables, lemon yogurt, and herbs. Warm one part, keep one part raw, and the bowl has contrast.”

“Dinner seven is intentionally flexible. Press the final rice into the hot skillet until the bottom gets crisp. Top it with a fried egg, the last protein, or a spoon of yogurt and herbs. It is not a sad leftover plate; it is a new texture and a clean fridge.”

**[11:10–12:30 | Taste, storage, and close]**

**VISUAL:** Seven labeled meal cards appear. Show portions in containers, then the clean skillet and sink.

**HARISH, on camera:**

“Here are the seven dinners: the lemon protein bowl, crispy chickpea rice, tomato eggs, fridge fried rice, chickpea tomato soup, the warm salad, and the crispy rice remix. The grocery list stayed small because the ingredients changed roles.”

“For storage, keep rice and cooked proteins cold in shallow containers, keep the yogurt sauce separate, and reheat until steaming. If something smells or looks wrong, do not taste-test it—discard it. A meal plan is only useful when it is safe and easy to follow.”

“The bigger lesson is to prep components, not seven finished meals. A base should save time without forcing you to eat the same flavor all week. # Long-form script 2: Five One-Pan Dinners With Almost No Cleanup

**Working title:** 5 One-Pan Dinners for a Tiny Kitchen (No Oven, No Pile of Dishes)

**Alternate titles:**

- Five One-Skillet Meals That Do Not Taste Like Shortcuts
- The Tiny-Kitchen One-Pan Dinner Test
- I Used One Pan for Five Complete Dinners

**Target runtime:** 11–13 minutes

**Thumbnail:** Five small plates arranged around one skillet; text: “1 PAN / 5 DINNERS”

**Episode promise:** Demonstrate five distinct formulas—pasta, rice, beans, eggs, and fish/tofu—while teaching pan management and cleanup timing.

### Script

**[0:00–0:25 | Cold open]**

**VISUAL:** Five finished dishes flash in quick cuts. A hand points to one skillet and one small cutting board. The empty sink remains visible.

**HARISH:**

“One pan, five dinners, and the sink is still visible. Today I’m testing whether a tiny kitchen can produce variety without turning cleanup into the second job. We have a tomato pasta, a spiced rice skillet, saucy beans, a vegetable egg dinner, and a fast fish or tofu pan. The rule is simple: each meal gets one cooking vessel, and each one must feel complete.”

**[0:25–1:10 | Rules and setup]**

**VISUAL:** Show skillet, lid, spatula, knife, cutting board, bowl, and a damp towel. A card reads “one pan / one board / one bowl.”

**HARISH:**

“A one-pan dinner does not mean throwing everything into a cold pan and hoping for the best. It means we manage heat and sequence so the same pan can brown, soften, simmer, and finish. I’m using a wide skillet with a lid. A Dutch oven or sauté pan works too. If your pan is smaller, make half batches rather than crowding it.”

“The ingredient group is flexible: onion, garlic, one or two vegetables, canned tomatoes, pasta, rice, beans, eggs, and either fish or tofu. We will use flavor switches—cumin and chili for one pan, lemon and herbs for another—so the meals do not taste like the same sauce five times.”

**[1:10–3:05 | Dinner one: one-pan tomato pasta]**

**VISUAL:** Oil, sliced garlic, onion, chili flakes, dry pasta, tomatoes, water. Use a measuring cup to add liquid.

**HARISH:**

“Dinner one is one-pan tomato pasta. Start with oil, then onion and a pinch of salt. Salt early helps the onion soften, but do not add so much that you cannot adjust later. When the onion is translucent, add garlic and chili for thirty seconds. Garlic burns quickly, so it gets less time than the onion.”

“Add dry pasta, crushed tomatoes, and enough water or stock to almost cover the noodles. Stir, bring it to a simmer, then cover. The pasta releases starch as it cooks, which thickens the sauce in the same pan. That is the reason this works.”

**VISUAL:** Lid lift, stir from bottom, add greens. Close-up of sauce coating noodles.

**HARISH:**

“Stir every couple of minutes, especially around the edges. If the pan looks dry before the noodles are tender, add a small splash of water. If the sauce is loose at the end, cook uncovered for another minute. Finish with greens, lemon, black pepper, and cheese if you have it.”

“The texture test is more useful than the clock: bite a noodle. It should be tender with just a little resistance. Transfer this to a plate or container before starting the next meal, then scrape the pan while it is still warm. Warm residue comes away easily; cold tomato glue becomes a project.”

**[3:05–4:45 | Dinner two: spiced chickpea rice skillet]**

**VISUAL:** Wipe pan, add onion and carrot, spices bloom, rice and chickpeas, stock, lid.

**HARISH:**

“Dinner two uses the same pan, but we change the method. Add onion and carrot with a little oil. Once they soften, add cumin, paprika, and black pepper directly to the oil. Spices taste fuller when they bloom for a few seconds.”

“Stir in rinsed rice and chickpeas so every grain gets coated. Add stock, bring it to a simmer, cover, and lower the heat. Resist lifting the lid every thirty seconds. Rice needs steady steam. Use that time to wash the knife and clear the board.”

**VISUAL:** After cooking, rest with lid on, fluff with fork, fold in greens and lemon.

**HARISH:**

“When the liquid is absorbed, turn off the heat and leave the lid on for five minutes. That rest lets the steam finish the center of the rice. Fluff with a fork, then fold in greens and lemon. Add yogurt at the table, not into the hot pan, if you want a cool contrast.”

**[4:45–6:25 | Dinner three: saucy white beans and toast substitute]**

**VISUAL:** Pan with garlic, tomatoes, beans, greens. A small tortilla or toast warms on the side only if desired; emphasize pan meal can stand alone.

**HARISH:**

“Dinner three is saucy beans. This is the fast pantry dinner for the night when the fridge is almost empty. Sauté garlic and chili in oil, add tomatoes, then beans. Mash a few beans against the pan. That thickens the sauce without cream or extra equipment.”

“Add greens and simmer until just wilted. Taste before adding lemon because canned beans and stock can already be salty. Eat it as a bowl, spoon it over toast, or wrap it in a tortilla. The meal is complete because it has protein, sauce, and a vegetable; the extra starch is optional.”

**VISUAL:** Spoon presses beans, sauce thickens, lemon squeezed over bowl.

**HARISH:**

“The useful one-pan lesson here is controlled mashing. You are not turning everything into purée. You are creating a few creamy pockets while leaving enough whole beans for texture.”

**[6:25–8:05 | Dinner four: vegetables and eggs]**

**VISUAL:** Carrot or potato slices, bell pepper, zucchini, or frozen vegetables. Cook covered, make wells, add eggs.

**HARISH:**

“Dinner four is vegetables and eggs. Use the vegetables you actually have. Hard vegetables go in first with a splash of water and the lid. Softer vegetables go in later so they do not disappear.”

“When the vegetables are nearly tender, push them aside and create two wells. Crack in the eggs, season the eggs directly, cover, and cook until the whites are set. Add a spoon of yogurt, herbs, or hot sauce at the end.”

**HARISH, on camera:**

“If the eggs cook too quickly on the bottom, lower the heat and add a teaspoon of water before covering. The steam cooks the top while the gentler heat protects the base. This is a good example of using the lid as a tiny oven.”

**[8:05–9:45 | Dinner five: lemon fish or tofu pan]**

**VISUAL:** Pat fish dry or press tofu. Season. Sear in clean pan. Add tomatoes or greens, lemon, and cover briefly.

**HARISH:**

“The fifth dinner is lemon fish or tofu. Pat fish dry; press tofu between towels if you have time. Season with salt, pepper, paprika, and a little flour or cornstarch if you like a crisp edge.”

“Sear the protein without moving it until it releases from the pan. If it sticks, it probably needs another moment. Flip, add greens or thin vegetables, then add lemon juice and a splash of water. Cover briefly to finish.”

“For fish, cook until the center is opaque and flakes easily. For tofu, cook until it is hot through and the outside is browned. Use the same lemon pan juices as the sauce. This is the moment when a one-pan meal feels less like a compromise and more like a restaurant trick.”

**[9:45–10:55 | Cleanup system]**

**VISUAL:** Show the “three-minute reset”: transfer food, soak spatula, wipe board, rinse pan, wipe counter.

**HARISH:**

“Here is how the sink stayed reasonable. First, transfer the food immediately. Second, put the spatula and any sticky utensil into warm water. Third, wipe the board while the pan is still warm. Fourth, rinse the pan—do not shock a very hot pan with cold water if the manufacturer says not to—and use a soft sponge.”

“The goal is not to pretend cleanup takes zero minutes. The goal is to make it predictable. If cleanup has a beginning and an end, it is much easier to start dinner.”

**[10:55–12:00 | Taste test and close]**

**VISUAL:** Five dishes in a row with labels: tomato pasta, chickpea rice, saucy beans, eggs and vegetables, lemon fish/tofu.

**HARISH:**

“The five winners are different because the pan changed jobs: simmering pasta, steaming rice, reducing beans, steaming eggs, and searing a protein. The small kitchen does not remove options; it makes sequencing more important.”

“If you are starting tonight, choose the one-pan meal that matches your energy. Pasta for comfort, beans for the pantry, eggs for speed, rice for leftovers, fish or tofu when you want a clean, bright plate. Tell me which one you want in a full recipe video. Subscribe for more small-space meals, and I’ll see you next time.”

**END SCREEN:** “Which one-pan dinner wins? Comment 1, 2, 3, 4, or 5.”
